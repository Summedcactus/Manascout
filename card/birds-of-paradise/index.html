MTG PRINTING GUIDE

Birds of Paradise

Birds of Paradise reaches all the way back to Magic’s original Limited Edition Alpha release in 1993. Its printing history lets you choose between Mark Poole’s early image, Edward P. Beard, Jr.’s Seventh Edition interpretation, Marcelo Vignali’s Ravnica-era art and later borderless work. The useful question is which painting and physical edition you want, with foil considered separately.

When was Birds of Paradise first printed?

◇

Birds of Paradise first appeared in Limited Edition Alpha (LEA), 5 August 1993. The original record is LEA #186, at rare, illustrated by Mark Poole. Original finish availability: nonfoil only.

The original edition predates printed collector numbers; the number above identifies the catalogue record.

HOW IT WORKS

One green mana, a flying Bird and five-colour fixing

◇

Birds of Paradise costs {G}, is a 0/1 Creature — Bird with flying, and taps for one mana of any colour. Its colour identity is green: producing mana of any colour does not make the card five-colour.

The tap ability is subject to the normal restriction on activating a creature’s {T} abilities before you have controlled it continuously since your turn began, unless it has haste. It fixes mana colours rather than producing several mana at once; that distinguishes it from the creature-count scaling of Gaea’s Cradle.

PRINTING HISTORY

An Alpha original, early core-set foils and modern frame choices

◇

Alpha (LEA) #186 debuted on 5 August 1993 at rare, illustrated by Mark Poole and available only in nonfoil. #186 is a catalogue identifier; the physical Alpha card has no printed collector number. Beta, Unlimited and later early core sets gave the familiar card distinct edition identities.

Seventh Edition in 2001 is an important artwork and surface comparison. Edward P. Beard, Jr. illustrates both the nonfoil #231 and separately catalogued foil #231★. The nonfoil has a white border; the foil has a black border. These are original-frame cards from a later edition, not Alpha foils.

Ravnica: City of Guilds #153 in 2005 introduces Marcelo Vignali’s image in a modern frame, with nonfoil and traditional foil. The Magic 2011 Buy-a-Box promo #165★ instead credits Terese Nielsen and is foil-only. Do not confuse that promotional painting with regular M11 #165 by Vignali.

Dominaria Remastered in January 2023 offers regular #151 and retro-frame #336 credited to Poole, plus Darrell Riche’s borderless #439. They are same-release variants. The retro frame on #336 does not turn the copy into an early core-set printing.

This is a representative history rather than a checklist of every core set, Commander deck, Secret Lair or crossover. The shared explorer supplies the larger catalogue, including collector-product and digital entries that require their own product checks.

ARTWORK & FRAMES

The artist and frame tell different parts of the story

◇

Mark Poole: Alpha is the historical anchor. His Dominaria Remastered entries let you compare a later regular frame with a later retro frame. Matching an artist credit does not by itself identify the edition or establish an original Alpha copy.

Edward P. Beard, Jr.: Seventh Edition’s illustration is its own visual era. Its white-border nonfoil and black-border foil use the same Seventh Edition painting, so the premium decision changes the surface and border without changing the artist.

Marcelo Vignali: RAV #153 is a clear alternative to the early Poole and Beard presentations. For an M11 copy, check whether you are viewing Vignali’s ordinary card or Nielsen’s Buy-a-Box art.

Terese Nielsen and Darrell Riche: PM11 #165★ is a specific promo image; DMR #439 is a later borderless alternative. “Premium Birds” does not identify either one precisely.

FOILS & PREMIUM FINISHES

Seventh Edition foil, Buy-a-Box foil or a later retro foil?

◇

Alpha #186 is nonfoil-only. The Seventh Edition foil has its own starred catalogue entry, #231★, while #231 is nonfoil-only. The star helps identify the catalogue record; actual photographs, borders and edition details still matter when examining a physical copy.

RAV #153 and the three shortlisted DMR records offer nonfoil and traditional foil. PM11 #165★ is foil-only. If you want Nielsen’s Buy-a-Box illustration, a nonfoil regular M11 card is not the same printing.

The original-frame Seventh Edition foil and the retro-frame DMR foil are different collector goals. A newer retro frame is not a first-edition foil, and an original-art preference does not establish that every edition has a reflective counterpart.

WHICH PRINTING?

Which Birds of Paradise printing should you choose?

◇

For the first printing: LEA #186 is the original. Identify early editions using their physical characteristics rather than a catalogue number or shared image alone.

For Seventh Edition collecting: compare #231 nonfoil and #231★ foil. The border difference is part of the choice, and the actual foil’s condition deserves close inspection.

For a conventional modern-frame deck copy: RAV #153 is a useful artwork-led reference alongside later regular reprints. Compare the current offer and condition rather than assuming one set is always cheapest.

For a specific illustration: choose PM11 #165★ for Nielsen or DMR #439 for Riche. If a retro frame matters most, DMR #336 is the relevant later record.

ManaScout pick: Seventh Edition beside Dominaria Remastered

◇

Compare 7ED #231 nonfoil with DMR #336 nonfoil first. Beard’s white-border core-set presentation and Poole’s later retro-frame record make two different kinds of nostalgia visible without a foil premium deciding the result.

For a borderless collection, #439 is our next comparison because Riche supplies a genuinely different illustration. The right choice depends on the artistic era you want to keep, not an assumed investment ranking.
